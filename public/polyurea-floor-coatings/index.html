Polyurea floor coatings · Anthem, AZ

A tough, elastic foundation for floors that work hard.

Polyurea bonds tightly, flexes with the slab and absorbs impact — the base layer we reach for in shops, RV bays and detached buildings.

What polyurea brings to a floor

Polyurea is best known for spray-on truck bed liners and protective coatings on tanks and pipelines. Its reputation comes from two properties: it bonds aggressively, and it stretches without tearing. The floor version is made to be spread by roller or squeegee, so garages and shops get the same rugged, rubbery resilience.

In a floor system, polyurea usually takes the role of base coat. It wets into the ground concrete, grips tightly and cures quickly into a resilient film. Decorative flake or pigment goes into or onto it, and a clear polyaspartic topcoat finishes the system. The result is a floor that can flex a little with the slab and absorb knocks that might crack a more rigid coating.

Where polyurea earns its place

  • Hobby and woodworking shops where tools and materials hit the floor regularly
  • Bays for RVs, boats, trailers and toy haulers with heavy, turning tires
  • Detached garages and casitas-in-progress without air conditioning
  • Garages with existing hairline cracks or a history of movement
  • Projects that need to be finished quickly in hot or cool weather

Key advantages

Elastic

Stretches with small slab movements from heat and settling instead of cracking.

Impact-resistant

Absorbs dropped wrenches, hitches and jack handles better than brittle films.

Aggressive bond

Grips deeply into properly ground concrete.

Fast cure

Shortens downtime, especially with a polyaspartic top.

Temperature tolerant

Can be applied in a wider range of conditions than many epoxies.

Common polyurea systems

Polyurea + flake + polyaspartic

Our most popular combination for active garages: flexible, decorative and sun-stable.

Pigmented polyurea + clear top

A solid gray or tan shop floor that makes dropped hardware easy to spot.

Heavy-duty shop build

Extra build and aggregate in high-impact zones for engine stands, jacks and rolling chests.

Textured wash-bay finish

Coarser aggregate where vehicles and gear are washed and traction is the priority.

Installation steps

  1. 01

    Read the slab

    We look at crack patterns, joints and any settling to understand how the concrete moves.

  2. 02

    Grind for bond

    A thorough grind leaves an open profile for polyurea to grip. Post-tension slabs are ground at the surface only.

  3. 03

    Address cracks and joints

    Stable cracks are filled; active joints are treated individually with the right filler.

  4. 04

    Apply the base

    Polyurea goes down in small, timed batches. Flake is broadcast immediately.

  5. 05

    Topcoat

    Once cured, excess flake is removed and a polyaspartic topcoat is applied.

  6. 06

    Return to work

    Foot traffic usually resumes the next day; vehicles and equipment after a few days.

Experience with fast-reacting materials

Polyurea’s quick reaction leaves little room for error. Measurements must be precise, mixing thorough and spreading immediate. The concrete has to be clean and dry, because polyurea is unforgiving of contamination. Not every product sold as polyurea is designed for floors, either; some are meant for spraying with specialized equipment. Our crews know which formulations perform on garage slabs and how to apply them evenly.

We also give an honest assessment of the slab. When drainage or shifting soil is pulling a crack wider, no coating will cure it, and you’ll hear that from us up front.

A flexible base won’t stop a slab from moving. It helps the coating move with it.

Polyurea in Anthem and the foothills

Custom properties in Desert Hills and New River often include detached garages, RV barns and workshops. These buildings rarely have air conditioning, so their slabs swing through a wider temperature range than an attached garage. That repeated expansion and contraction is exactly what a flexible base is designed to handle.

Even in Anthem’s master-planned neighborhoods, many garages double as workshops or hold boats and trailers. Those bays reward a rubbery base under a thicker top layer, which resists the scrub of loaded tires turning in place.

How polyurea fits with epoxy and polyaspartic

Epoxy is rigid, economical and an excellent bonder, but it yellows in the sun. Polyurea is flexible and fast, making it a strong base where movement or impact is expected. Polyaspartic, a polyurea relative, stays clear under UV, making it the go-to topcoat. Good floors frequently combine two of the three. During your quote we’ll explain the combination we recommend and why.

Signs your garage would benefit from a flexible base

Any of these can still be coated; they just point toward a stretchier base for a longer life.

  • Hairline cracks running across the slab, especially near corners or the garage door
  • Previous paint that split exactly where the slab split
  • Chipped or crumbling control joint edges
  • A detached building without air conditioning
  • Heavy items regularly dropped, dragged or rolled across the floor

Boats, trailers and toy haulers

Anthem’s location makes it easy to get to Lake Pleasant, the trails around New River and the high country up I-17, so plenty of garages hold boats, side-by-sides and trailers. These loads are hard on floors in a specific way: heavy weight sits on small tire patches, and when a trailer is jockeyed into position, those tires twist against the coating.

A polyurea base with a heavier topcoat handles that twisting better than a brittle film. We also recommend a pair of rubber mats under trailer tires that stay parked for long periods, and a pad under the tongue jack.

What to expect during the quote

We’ll start by looking closely at the concrete: how cracks have formed, whether joint edges are chipped, whether old coatings are lifting and what the moisture readings say. Then we’ll ask how the space is used — what’s parked, what’s stored, whether you work on projects.

That lets us say plainly whether the upgrade earns its keep or a standard epoxy base is enough. The reasoning goes into your written quote so you can make an informed decision.

Caring for a polyurea floor

Upkeep matches any of our floors — a broom, quick spill cleanup and an occasional gentle mopping. Put plywood under jack stands or engine stands that will sit for weeks to spread the load, and use a fire-resistant blanket under welding or grinding work.

Detached shops and RV barns

Custom lots in Desert Hills and New River often include steel buildings, RV barns or detached garages that sit closed up through summer. Inside, temperatures can climb well beyond the outdoors, and the slab expands and contracts more than an air-conditioned space. Over many seasons, rigid coatings in these buildings are more prone to cracking at joints and lifting near doors.

A polyurea base is designed for exactly this environment. Combined with a heavier topcoat and properly treated joints, it gives a shop or barn floor its best chance of lasting through years of temperature swings and heavy use.

The cost question

Polyurea costs more than a standard epoxy base, both for materials and because the fast cure requires an experienced, coordinated crew. Whether that extra cost makes sense depends on the building and how you use it. For a stable, insulated attached garage with light use, an epoxy base is a sound choice. For a shop, RV barn or garage with active cracks, the added flexibility usually pays for itself by avoiding repairs. We’ll show you both options.

A simple way to think about it

If your garage is a stable, air-conditioned space used mostly for parking, an epoxy base will likely serve you well. If it’s a working shop, an RV barn or a building that bakes all summer, polyurea is worth considering. And if you’re not sure, that’s exactly what the free on-site quote is for.

Common questions

Polyurea vs. polyaspartic — what’s the difference?

Polyaspartic is a slower-curing, UV-stable member of the polyurea family. A common recipe pairs a polyurea foundation with a polyaspartic finish.

Does polyurea prevent cracks?

It lets the finish ride out minor shifts, though ground that keeps moving can still open new cracks. We repair cracks first and flag any that look active.

Is polyurea overkill for a newer house?

Not always. Many newer, stable garages do well with an epoxy base. We recommend polyurea where use or conditions call for it.

Is it more expensive?

Polyurea materials cost more than epoxy and require an experienced crew. You’ll see both priced side by side.

Can I weld on it?

Hot slag can mark any coating. Use a welding blanket in welding areas.

Can polyurea go outdoors?

As a base layer, yes, under a UV-stable topcoat. Bare polyurea can discolor in sunlight, so we always finish outdoor work with polyaspartic.
